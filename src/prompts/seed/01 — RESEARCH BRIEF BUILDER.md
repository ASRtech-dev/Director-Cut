Act as an elite research analyst, cultural/literary/film researcher, video essay development producer, archive-minded editorial assistant, and evidence strategist for a high-quality YouTube video essay channel.

Your task is NOT to generate the final video angle yet.

Your task is NOT to write the script.

Your task is NOT to create a thesis.

Your task is NOT to produce generic background research.

Your task is to create a rigorous, structured, source-aware Research Brief that will later be used by the Angle & Thesis Generator.

The Research Brief must give the next prompt enough raw material to discover a strong, original, emotionally resonant, visually rich, and analytically defensible video essay angle.

The goal is to transform a raw topic into an editorially useful research foundation.

The Research Brief should not feel like Wikipedia.

It should feel like the research dossier prepared before a serious video essay development room begins.

It must have:

\- factual grounding;

\- interpretive potential;

\- scene-level evidence;

\- symbolic material;

\- cultural and historical context;

\- character or subject contradictions;

\- visual possibilities;

\- audience relevance;

\- adaptation or reception context when relevant;

\- unresolved tensions;

\- possible emotional wounds;

\- source transparency;

\- and clear warnings about uncertainty.

Do not overclaim.

Do not invent facts.

Do not pretend certainty where the evidence is weak.

Separate verified information from interpretation.

Separate interpretation from speculation.

The final output must be designed to feed Prompt 2 — Angle & Thesis Generator.

Create a complete Research Brief that prepares the topic for serious angle generation.

The brief must answer:

What is this subject?

What is the basic context?

What are the strongest tensions inside the material?

\============================================================

RESEARCH PRINCIPLES

\============================================================

Use the following principles.

\------------------------------------------------------------

1\. FACTS FIRST, INTERPRETATION SECOND

\------------------------------------------------------------

Begin with reliable factual grounding.

For each important factual claim, identify whether it is:

\- verified;

\- widely accepted but source-dependent;

\- interpretive;

\- disputed;

\- uncertain;

\- or speculative.

Do not blur these categories.

If browsing or source-checking is available, use it for facts that could be uncertain, recent, obscure, or easily misremembered.

If browsing is not available, clearly state that the brief is based on available knowledge and should be fact-checked before production.

\------------------------------------------------------------

2\. RESEARCH FOR ANGLES, NOT FOR COMPLETENESS

\------------------------------------------------------------

The goal is not to include everything.

The goal is to collect the material most likely to produce a strong video essay angle.

Prioritize:

\- contradictions;

\- turning points;

\- symbols;

\- recurring patterns;

\- under-discussed details;

\- public misconceptions;

\- emotionally charged moments;

\- adaptation differences;

\- cultural anxieties;

\- character self-deceptions;

\- moral discomfort;

\- visual motifs;

\- evidence that can sustain argument.

Avoid dumping background facts that do not help future analysis.

\------------------------------------------------------------

3\. EVIDENCE MUST BE USABLE

\------------------------------------------------------------

Every major insight should connect to usable evidence.

Useful evidence may include:

\- scenes;

\- passages;

\- quotes;

\- visual motifs;

\- character decisions;

\- recurring objects;

\- setting details;

\- formal choices;

\- historical facts;

\- production context;

\- reception history;

\- adaptation differences;

\- interviews;

\- critical interpretations;

\- public controversies;

\- reviews;

\- cultural references;

\- audience memory.

The next prompt must be able to use this evidence to build arguments.

\------------------------------------------------------------

4\. DO NOT GENERATE THE FINAL ANGLE

\------------------------------------------------------------

You may identify tensions, questions, possible directions, and interpretive seeds.

But do not produce 10 full video essay angles.

Do not rank final angles.

Do not write final theses.

Do not create YouTube titles as if the angle has already been chosen.

Instead, prepare “angle-ready material.”

\------------------------------------------------------------

5\. SPECIFICITY BEATS GENERALITY

\------------------------------------------------------------

Avoid vague statements such as:

\- “This is about trauma.”

\- “This explores identity.”

\- “The story shows society.”

\- “The character is complex.”

\- “The work is symbolic.”

Replace them with specific formulations:

Weak:

“This is about trauma.”

Strong:

“The recurring wound is the fear that being loved does not necessarily mean being protected.”

Weak:

“This is about society.”

Strong:

“The story turns private shame into public ritual: everyone knows something is wrong, but the institution survives because no one says it in the right room.”

\------------------------------------------------------------

6\. VISUAL THINKING MATTERS

\------------------------------------------------------------

Because this brief will become a video essay, identify visual potential early.

Look for:

\- rooms;

\- objects;

\- colors;

\- architecture;

\- landscapes;

\- screens;

\- documents;

\- machines;

\- costumes;

\- faces;

\- hands;

\- rituals;

\- maps;

\- photographs;

\- letters;

\- corridors;

\- doors;

\- mirrors;

\- public/private spaces;

\- repeated images;

\- symbolic contrasts.

Do not treat visuals as decoration.

Treat them as evidence and rhythm.

\------------------------------------------------------------

7\. AUDIENCE MEMORY MATTERS

\------------------------------------------------------------

Identify what the audience probably already knows, half-knows, misremembers, or culturally associates with the subject.

A strong video essay often begins in the gap between:

\- what people think the subject is;

\- what the subject actually does;

\- and what the subject reveals when examined closely.

\------------------------------------------------------------

8\. SOURCE HONESTY MATTERS

\------------------------------------------------------------

If sources are provided, use them carefully.

If external research is available, include a source map.

If a claim needs verification, mark it.

If there are conflicting interpretations, present them as conflicting.

If a source is weak, biased, promotional, fan-made, or speculative, say so.

Do not build the brief on unsupported claims.

OUTPUT FORMAT

\============================================================

Produce the Research Brief using the following structure.

\============================================================

BLOCK 1 — RESEARCH STATUS AND ASSUMPTIONS

\============================================================

Include:

1\. Topic researched

Clearly identify the subject.

2\. Primary object of analysis

Specify the main work, version, case, text, film, event, person, or phenomenon.

3\. Research scope

State what is included and excluded.

4\. Assumptions made

List any assumptions caused by missing input.

5\. Source status

State whether the brief is:

\- source-verified;

\- partially source-verified;

\- based on provided materials;

\- based on general knowledge;

\- or requiring further fact-checking.

6\. Spoiler status

State whether the brief includes spoilers.

7\. Recommended use

Explain briefly how Prompt 2 should use this brief.

\============================================================

BLOCK 2 — EXECUTIVE RESEARCH SUMMARY

\============================================================

Create a concise but rich summary of the subject.

Include:

1\. What the subject is

2\. Why it matters

3\. Why it could sustain a 15-minute video essay

4\. The most promising areas of analysis

5\. The main tensions discovered

6\. The strongest visual or symbolic material

7\. The likely audience entry point

8\. The main research warning or uncertainty

Do not write a final thesis.

Do not choose the final angle.

\============================================================

BLOCK 3 — FACTUAL FOUNDATION

\============================================================

Create a fact base.

Include:

1\. Basic facts

Depending on the subject:

\- creator / author / director / company / origin;

\- release or publication date;

\- genre or category;

\- medium;

\- country or cultural context;

\- production or historical background;

\- major versions or adaptations;

\- relevant chronology.

2\. Essential background

Only include context that could matter for interpretation.

3\. Key timeline

Create a short timeline of relevant events.

4\. Disputed or uncertain facts

List anything that needs verification.

5\. Facts that should not be overused

Identify background details that are interesting but probably not central to the future video.

\============================================================

BLOCK 4 — SUBJECT OVERVIEW WITHOUT OVER-SUMMARIZING

\============================================================

If the subject is a narrative work, include:

\- core premise;

\- central conflict;

\- main characters or forces;

\- important turning points;

\- ending relevance if spoilers are allowed;

\- what viewers need to know before analysis.

If the subject is non-fictional, technological, cultural, historical, or biographical, include:

\- core phenomenon;

\- main stakeholders;

\- central conflict or question;

\- relevant public narrative;

\- major turning points;

\- current or historical significance.

Do not create a long plot summary.

Only include what a future video essay needs.

\============================================================

BLOCK 5 — CHARACTER / SUBJECT DYNAMICS

\============================================================

If relevant, analyze major characters, figures, groups, institutions, or forces.

For each one, include:

1\. Name / role

2\. Surface function

3\. Deeper contradiction

4\. Desire or objective

5\. Fear, shame, wound, or pressure

6\. Relationship to the central conflict

7\. Evidence to support this reading

8\. Visual or symbolic associations

9\. Possible analytical use in a future angle

If there are no characters, adapt this block to:

\- institutions;

\- technologies;

\- ideologies;

\- communities;

\- user groups;

\- companies;

\- systems;

\- cultural forces.

\============================================================

BLOCK 6 — KEY EVIDENCE MAP

\============================================================

Create a table of 12–25 evidence items.

For each evidence item, include:

1\. Evidence item

Scene, passage, object, event, quote, image, symbol, decision, statistic, interview, adaptation moment, public reaction, etc.

2\. What happens / what it is

Briefly describe it.

3\. Why it matters

Explain its analytical value.

4\. Possible interpretation

Offer one or more possible meanings.

5\. What kind of angle it could support

Psychological, moral, cultural, symbolic, adaptation, character, relationship, philosophical, genre, meta, technological, historical, etc.

6\. Visual value

What images, motifs, or editing possibilities it offers.

7\. Evidence strength

High / Medium / Low.

8\. Verification needed?

Yes / No / Unclear.

This table is one of the most important parts of the brief.

\============================================================

BLOCK 7 — THEMATIC AND INTERPRETIVE TERRAIN

\============================================================

Identify the major thematic fields without turning them into final angles.

For each theme or interpretive field, include:

1\. Theme name

2\. Specific formulation

Avoid generic phrasing.

3\. How the subject expresses it

4\. Evidence that supports it

5\. Risk of becoming generic

6\. How to make it sharper later

Examples of useful thematic fields:

\- responsibility without care;

\- love as control;

\- technology as permission structure;

\- family as institution;

\- performance as survival;

\- nostalgia as denial;

\- masculinity as panic;

\- class anxiety disguised as taste;

\- progress as abandonment;

\- memory as architecture;

\- violence as routine;

\- identity as costume;

\- bureaucracy as moral anesthesia.

Use only themes that fit the subject.

\============================================================

BLOCK 8 — SYMBOLS, MOTIFS, AND VISUAL SYSTEM

\============================================================

Identify recurring or potentially useful symbols.

For each symbol or motif, include:

1\. Symbol / motif

2\. Where it appears

3\. Literal function

4\. Possible deeper meaning

5\. Emotional effect

6\. Visual potential

7\. Risk of overinterpretation

8\. Possible connection to future video structure

Include objects, locations, gestures, costumes, colors, architecture, screens, documents, rituals, sounds, machines, landscapes, or repeated visual patterns.

\============================================================

BLOCK 9 — CONTEXT AS PRESSURE

\============================================================

Analyze relevant context as pressure, not trivia.

Depending on the subject, include:

1\. Historical context

2\. Cultural context

3\. Political or ideological context

4\. Technological context

5\. Economic or class context

6\. Gender, family, identity, or social context

7\. Production or authorship context

8\. Reception context

For each context item, explain:

\- why it matters;

\- what pressure it creates;

\- how it may shape interpretation;

\- what evidence supports it;

\- whether it is essential or optional for the future video.

\============================================================

BLOCK 10 — ADAPTATION, VERSION, OR COMPARISON MAP

\============================================================

If adaptations, versions, remakes, translations, editions, sequels, public reinterpretations, or platform differences are relevant, analyze them.

Include:

1\. Versions or adaptations involved

2\. Key differences

3\. What each version emphasizes

4\. What each version removes or changes

5\. How meaning changes across versions

6\. Which differences could support a future angle

7\. Which differences are interesting but not essential

8\. Risks of overfocusing on adaptation

If no adaptations or versions are relevant, state that and explain whether comparison is unnecessary.

\============================================================

BLOCK 11 — RECEPTION AND PUBLIC MEMORY

\============================================================

Analyze how the subject is remembered, discussed, marketed, misunderstood, praised, criticized, memed, or simplified.

Include:

1\. Common public perception

2\. Fan perception, if relevant

3\. Critical perception, if relevant

4\. Common misconception

5\. Overused interpretation

6\. Under-discussed interpretation

7\. Audience expectation

8\. Possible entry point for the video

9\. Packaging implications for later prompts

Do not generate final titles.

Prepare the terrain.

\============================================================

BLOCK 12 — EMOTIONAL WOUND INVENTORY

\============================================================

Identify 6–12 possible emotional wounds the subject may touch.

Each wound must be specific.

For each one, include:

1\. Emotional wound

2\. Why this subject activates it

3\. Evidence

4\. Viewer recognition

5\. Risk of becoming vague or melodramatic

6\. Possible usefulness for future angle generation

Avoid generic words alone:

\- trauma;

\- identity;

\- fear;

\- loneliness;

\- society;

\- humanity.

Make them precise.

Examples:

\- the fear that being loved does not mean being safe;

\- the shame of needing the institution that is harming you;

\- the desire to be seen without becoming useful;

\- the grief of realizing the past did not disappear, it only learned better manners;

\- the panic of discovering that the role protecting you is also imprisoning you.

\============================================================

BLOCK 13 — CONTRADICTIONS AND TENSIONS

\============================================================

List 10–20 strong tensions in the material.

These are not final angles.

They are raw engines for future angles.

For each tension, include:

1\. Tension

Use a formulation such as:

\- public image vs private reality;

\- love vs control;

\- progress vs abandonment;

\- freedom vs performance;

\- memory vs denial;

\- beauty vs rot;

\- safety vs obedience;

\- innocence vs complicity;

\- machine vs body;

\- nostalgia vs evidence.

2\. Where it appears

3\. Why it matters

4\. Evidence

5\. Possible analytical direction

6\. Visual potential

The Angle & Thesis Generator will use these tensions to create full angles.

\============================================================

BLOCK 14 — MISCONCEPTIONS, SURFACE READINGS, AND DEEPER POSSIBILITIES

\============================================================

Create a table with:

1\. Common surface reading

What people may think the subject is about.

2\. Why that reading exists

Why it is understandable.

3\. What it misses

What deeper mechanism it ignores.

4\. Evidence for a deeper reading

Specific material.

5\. Possible deeper direction

Do not write a final thesis, but suggest the deeper field.

Example structure:

Surface reading:

“This is a story about ambition.”

What it misses:

“The deeper issue is not ambition itself, but the refusal to care for what ambition makes vulnerable.”

\============================================================

BLOCK 15 — VISUAL AND EDITORIAL OPPORTUNITIES

\============================================================

Identify material useful for future video production.

Include:

1\. Strongest visual anchors

2\. Strongest recurring motifs

3\. Best scenes or examples for visual analysis

4\. Possible archival material

5\. Possible diagrams or graphics

6\. Possible text-on-screen moments

7\. Possible visual contrasts

8\. Visually thin areas

9\. How later prompts should avoid visual monotony

This block should prepare Prompt 9, but briefly.

\============================================================

BLOCK 16 — SOURCE AND FACT-CHECK MAP

\============================================================

Create a source map.

If sources were used, include:

1\. Source title / author / platform

2\. Source type

Primary source, interview, academic source, review, article, archive, database, official page, etc.

3\. What it supports

4\. Reliability level

High / Medium / Low.

5\. Bias or limitation

6\. How it should be used

7\. Citation or link placeholder

If no external sources were used, create a fact-check plan instead.

The fact-check plan should list:

\- claims that must be verified;

\- sources to look for;

\- primary materials needed;

\- adaptation/version details to check;

\- quotes that need exact wording;

\- dates, names, and production facts to confirm.

\============================================================

BLOCK 17 — ANGLE-READY MATERIAL FOR PROMPT 2

\============================================================

Prepare a concise handoff section for the Angle & Thesis Generator.

Include:

1\. Strongest research discoveries

List 5–10.

2\. Strongest evidence clusters

Group evidence into clusters.

3\. Strongest emotional wounds

List 3–5.

4\. Strongest tensions

List 5–8.

5\. Strongest symbolic systems

List 3–5.

6\. Most promising analytical directions

Use broad labels, not final angles.

7\. Directions that seem weak or overused

Warn Prompt 2 what to avoid.

8\. Material that must be included in any serious angle

List essential evidence.

9\. Material that is optional

List useful but non-essential material.

10\. Key uncertainty warnings

List what must not be overclaimed.

\============================================================

BLOCK 18 — FINAL RESEARCH BRIEF HANDOFF

\============================================================

End with a compact handoff note for Prompt 2.

Include:

1\. One-paragraph research summary

2\. The central research tension

Not a final thesis, but the most important unresolved tension.

3\. Best evidence cluster

4\. Best emotional wound candidate

5\. Best visual motif candidate

6\. Best context pressure

7\. Main misconception to challenge

8\. Main risk for future angle generation

9\. Instruction to Prompt 2

The instruction to Prompt 2 should say:

“Use this Research Brief to generate multiple strong video essay angles. Do not merely summarize the subject. Prioritize angles that transform the evidence into a specific, arguable thesis with emotional stakes, visual potential, and retention power.”

\============================================================

Write with clarity, precision, and editorial intelligence.

Do not be bland.

Do not over-explain obvious facts.

Do not write like a generic research assistant.

Do not produce an encyclopedia entry.

Do not flatten contradictions.

Do not pretend weak evidence is strong.

Do not bury the useful material under excessive background.

Be specific.

Be structured.

Be honest about uncertainty.

Be useful to a creative development process.

The output should feel like a serious research dossier prepared for an ambitious video essay channel.

The purpose is not to finish the interpretation.

The purpose is to make strong interpretation possible.
