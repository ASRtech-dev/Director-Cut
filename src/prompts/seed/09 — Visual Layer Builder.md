CORE OBJECTIVE

\============================================================

Create a complete visual production plan for the final script.

The plan must:

\- preserve the script text;

\- divide the script into visual beats;

\- identify what each beat needs visually;

\- suggest concrete visuals;

\- suggest symbolic visuals;

\- suggest B-roll;

\- suggest archival or source-material references where relevant;

\- suggest text-on-screen moments if useful;

\- identify rhythm changes;

\- flag visually thin passages;

\- propose solutions for abstract sections;

\- maintain tonal consistency;

\- avoid visual clichés where possible;

\- support retention.

Do not rewrite the script unless you identify a minor visual problem that requires a tiny wording note.

If a passage is visually difficult, do not change it automatically.

Flag it and propose visual strategies.

\============================================================

VISUAL PRINCIPLES

\============================================================

Use the following principles.

\------------------------------------------------------------

1\. VISUALS MUST SERVE THE ARGUMENT

\------------------------------------------------------------

Do not suggest images just because they are pretty.

Each visual must clarify, intensify, complicate, or embody the argument.

A visual can serve:

\- evidence;

\- atmosphere;

\- irony;

\- emotional contrast;

\- symbolic meaning;

\- rhythm;

\- retention;

\- transition;

\- memory.

If it does none of these, do not include it.

\------------------------------------------------------------

2\. AVOID LITERALISM WHEN IT WEAKENS THE VIDEO

\------------------------------------------------------------

Do not always show exactly what the narration says.

Sometimes the best visual is:

\- a contrast;

\- a metaphor;

\- an object;

\- a texture;

\- a document;

\- an empty space;

\- a face;

\- a gesture;

\- architecture;

\- a repeated motif;

\- a pattern;

\- a symbolic cutaway.

Bad:

Narration says “fear” → show someone scared.

Better:

Narration says “fear” → show a closed door, a hallway light left on, a hand hovering near a phone, an empty chair at a family table.

\------------------------------------------------------------

3\. VISUAL MOTIFS CREATE COHERENCE

\------------------------------------------------------------

Identify recurring visual motifs.

Examples:

\- doors;

\- corridors;

\- windows;

\- mirrors;

\- documents;

\- receipts;

\- machines;

\- weather;

\- hands;

\- faces partly obscured;

\- family tables;

\- institutional spaces;

\- old photographs;

\- clocks;

\- maps;

\- keys;

\- empty rooms;

\- uniforms;

\- screens;

\- handwriting;

\- stains;

\- tools;

\- roads;

\- rituals.

Use motifs to make the video feel coherent.

Do not introduce a new visual language every 30 seconds.

\------------------------------------------------------------

4\. RHYTHM MATTERS

\------------------------------------------------------------

Visual rhythm should follow narrative rhythm.

Use:

\- slower visuals for emotional or reflective passages;

\- sharper cuts for punchlines or argument turns;

\- recurring images for thematic return;

\- visual contrast for counterpoint;

\- visual tightening before midpoint;

\- visual release or stillness near the closing.

Do not over-edit serious passages.

Do not leave abstract analysis visually static for too long.

\------------------------------------------------------------

5\. VISUALS SHOULD SUPPORT RETENTION

\------------------------------------------------------------

Every 30–60 seconds, the viewer should receive a visual or conceptual reset.

This may be:

\- a new image type;

\- a symbolic object;

\- a scene reference;

\- a text card;

\- an archival detail;

\- a diagram;

\- a contrast;

\- a repeated motif returning with new meaning;

\- a visual question;

\- a shift in pace.

Retention does not mean constant movement.

It means controlled attention.

\------------------------------------------------------------

6\. RESPECT THE WORK AND THE TONE

\------------------------------------------------------------

If analyzing a serious subject, do not trivialize it visually.

If humor appears, support it with precision, not meme editing.

Avoid:

\- cheap stock footage;

\- obvious reaction images;

\- random memes;

\- excessive zooms;

\- generic dramatic footage;

\- visual jokes that break the spell;

\- on-screen text that explains what the narration already says;

\- overused images unless deliberately recontextualized.

\------------------------------------------------------------

7\. VISUALIZE ABSTRACT IDEAS THROUGH CONCRETE SYSTEMS

\------------------------------------------------------------

For abstract concepts, use concrete visual systems.

Examples:

\- guilt → handwritten apology, unopened letter, mirror, stain, empty chair;

\- power → desk, corridor, uniform, title plaque, locked door;

\- denial → clean surface, covered furniture, closed curtains, white noise, repeated routine;

\- memory → old photographs, damaged tape, archive boxes, marked calendar;

\- addiction → glass, ritual, waiting hand, empty bottle, repeated pattern, receipt;

\- bureaucracy → forms, stamps, folders, fluorescent hallways, waiting rooms;

\- family tension → dinner table, doorframes, separate rooms, half-lit faces.

BLOCK 3 — BEAT-BY-BEAT VISUAL PLAN

\============================================================

Divide the script into manageable visual beats.

Each beat should usually be 15–45 seconds of narration.

For each beat, include:

1\. Beat number

2\. Script excerpt

Quote a short identifying phrase from the script, not the whole paragraph.

3\. Narrative purpose

What the beat is doing in the argument.

4\. Primary visual suggestion

Concrete visual idea.

5\. Secondary visual alternative

A backup or variation.

6\. Visual type

Choose one or more:

\- primary source clip/image;

\- archival image;

\- stock footage;

\- original filmed material;

\- symbolic B-roll;

\- text on screen;

\- simple motion graphic;

\- diagram;

\- still image;

\- AI-generated symbolic image;

\- abstract texture;

\- map/timeline;

\- object close-up.

7\. Emotional intention

What the viewer should feel.

8\. Retention function

How this visual beat keeps attention.

9\. Editing rhythm

Choose:

\- slow;

\- medium;

\- sharp;

\- montage;

\- stillness;

\- contrast cut;

\- rhythmic repetition.

10\. Notes / cautions

Mention copyright risk, cliché risk, tonal risk, or clarity risk.

Important:

Do not paste the entire script into this table.

Use short excerpts only.

BLOCK 6 — EVIDENCE AND SOURCE VISUALS

\============================================================

Identify which visuals should come from the primary work or factual sources.

For each evidence point, include:

1\. Evidence

Scene, passage, image, object, event, document, historical context, adaptation moment.

2\. Why it matters

Primary source, public archive, still image, official trailer, book cover, interview, map, document, etc.

Copyright, fair use, attribution, overuse, availability, ambiguity.

BLOCK 7 — ABSTRACT PASSAGES RESCUE PLAN

\============================================================

Identify passages that may be visually difficult because they are abstract, conceptual, or emotionally internal.

For each, include:

1\. Abstract passage or idea

2\. Why it is visually difficult

3\. Visual strategy 1

4\. Visual strategy 2

5\. Visual strategy 3

6\. Whether the script wording should be slightly adjusted

Only recommend tiny wording adjustments if truly needed.

\============================================================

BLOCK 8 — RETENTION AND PACING PLAN

\============================================================

Create a retention map.

Include:

1\. First 60 seconds

What visual choices should prevent early drop-off?

2\. Every 2–3 minute reset

Identify where the video should visually shift.

3\. Midpoint visual escalation

How should the midpoint feel visually different?

4\. Counterpoint visual shift

How should the counterpoint visually contrast the main argument?

5\. Closing visual strategy

How should the ending slow down, echo motifs, or leave a final image?

6\. Overstimulation risks

Where the edit should resist doing too much.

7\. Understimulation risks

Where the edit needs stronger visual support.

\============================================================

BLOCK 9 — SEARCH AND ASSET LIST

\============================================================

Create a practical asset search list.

Organize by category:

1\. Primary work visuals

Scenes, stills, covers, posters, trailers, official materials.

2\. Archival / historical visuals

Relevant time period, locations, interviews, newspaper clippings, maps, documents.

3\. Symbolic B-roll

Objects, rooms, gestures, weather, machines, roads, rituals, textures.

4\. Original graphics

Timelines, diagrams, comparison cards, quote cards, thematic maps.

5\. Possible AI-generated symbolic images

Only if allowed.

Describe images clearly but avoid relying on them for factual evidence.

6\. Audio-visual atmosphere references

Optional: mood references for pacing, not specific music instructions unless requested.

For each asset, include:

\- search keywords;

\- purpose;

\- priority level: High / Medium / Low;

\- caution.

STYLE REQUIREMENTS

\============================================================

Be concrete.

Be practical.

Be visually intelligent.

Do not write vague suggestions like:

\- “use dramatic visuals”;

\- “show emotion”;

\- “add cinematic shots”;

\- “use relevant clips.”

Always specify what kind of image, object, scene, texture, document, contrast, or motif.

Do not overdo visuals.

Do not recommend random montage.

Do not make the video look like a trailer unless the script demands it.

Support the argument.

Support the feeling.

Support the rhythm.

The visual layer should feel like a second essay running under the narration.
