It should not feel stitched together.

It should not feel like a compromise.

It should feel like the version the previous drafts were trying to become.

The script must have teeth.

Not noise.

Teeth.

\============================================================

MASTER CUT PRINCIPLES

\============================================================

Use the following principles.

\------------------------------------------------------------

1\. DO NOT AVERAGE

\------------------------------------------------------------

A Master Cut is not a democratic blend.

Do not include material just because it exists.

Choose the best base structure.

Import only what improves the final script.

Cut everything else.

A mediocre compromise is worse than a decisive version.

\------------------------------------------------------------

2\. ONE VOICE

\------------------------------------------------------------

The final script must sound like one narrator wrote it.

Even if material comes from multiple versions, rewrite it so the voice is consistent.

Avoid:

\- sudden tonal shifts;

\- one paragraph sounding academic and the next sounding comedic;

\- repeated thesis formulations;

\- inconsistent metaphor families;

\- mixed levels of irony;

\- competing emotional registers.

The narrator should feel distinctive, controlled, intelligent, and alive.

\------------------------------------------------------------

3\. THESIS FIDELITY

\------------------------------------------------------------

Preserve the approved thesis.

Do not change the meaning in pursuit of style.

Every section must serve the central argument.

If a line is clever but does not serve the thesis, cut it.

If a paragraph is beautiful but does not move the argument, cut or rewrite it.

\------------------------------------------------------------

4\. ARGUMENT MOVEMENT

\------------------------------------------------------------

The script must move.

It should not feel like:

\- a list of observations;

\- a collection of good paragraphs;

\- an essay with occasional jokes;

\- a plot summary with commentary;

\- disconnected thematic reflections.

The argument should escalate:

\- surface misconception;

\- central contradiction;

\- evidence;

\- mechanism;

\- deeper midpoint turn;

\- complication;

\- emotional landing.

The viewer should feel the analysis tightening.

\------------------------------------------------------------

5\. CONCRETE EVIDENCE

\------------------------------------------------------------

Use specific evidence.

Depending on the subject, include:

\- scenes;

\- passages;

\- symbols;

\- objects;

\- character decisions;

\- formal choices;

\- historical context;

\- adaptation differences;

\- recurring motifs;

\- public reaction;

\- creator decisions.

Do not let the script float in abstraction.

Every major claim should be anchored.

\------------------------------------------------------------

6\. RETENTION THROUGH RHYTHM

\------------------------------------------------------------

The script must hold attention through:

\- strong openings;

\- paragraph landings;

\- short sentences after dense passages;

\- controlled humor;

\- vivid comparisons;

\- concrete examples;

\- meaningful transitions;

\- emotional turns;

\- midpoint deepening;

\- counterpoint tension;

\- final resonance.

Every 45–60 seconds, include a retention beat:

\- a memorable line;

\- a sharp question;

\- a reversal;

\- a darkly funny observation;

\- a specific example;

\- a vivid image;

\- an emotional precision;

\- a symbolic interpretation.

Do not let any section become flat.

\------------------------------------------------------------

7\. PUNCHLINES MUST FEEL DISCOVERED

\------------------------------------------------------------

Use the strongest lines from the evaluated scripts only if they feel natural.

Revise them if needed.

Avoid:

\- slogan stacking;

\- trailer copy;

\- lines that sound like they were generated for a “memorable line” category;

\- forced symmetry;

\- too many “X is not Y, it is Z” structures.

The best punchlines should feel like the argument suddenly became sharper.

\------------------------------------------------------------

8\. EMOTIONAL RESTRAINT

\------------------------------------------------------------

The script should be emotionally resonant, not melodramatic.

Do not tell the viewer what to feel.

Create the conditions for the feeling.

Use:

\- concrete images;

\- precise contradictions;

\- human stakes;

\- restraint;

\- final lines that land cleanly.

\------------------------------------------------------------

9\. HUMOR AS DIAGNOSIS

\------------------------------------------------------------

Humor should sharpen the analysis.

Use humor through:

\- irony;

\- false politeness;

\- institutional language;

\- absurd contrast;

\- precise diagnosis;

\- exposure of self-deception.

Do not add jokes for decoration.

If a joke could be removed without weakening the idea, remove or rewrite it.

\------------------------------------------------------------

10\. VISUAL THINKING WITHOUT PRODUCTION NOTES

\------------------------------------------------------------

The Master Cut should be visually rich through narration.

Do not include:

\- visual directions;

\- shot descriptions;

\- camera notes;

\- sound cues;

\- editing notes;

\- production instructions.

But write in a way that gives the future visual layer strong material:

\- rooms;

\- objects;

\- gestures;

\- documents;

\- machines;

\- landscapes;

\- public/private contrast;

\- recurring motifs;

\- bodies in space;

\- symbolic images.

\============================================================

SECTION REQUIREMENTS

\============================================================

\------------------------------------------------------------

1\. HOOK / STRONG OPENING

\------------------------------------------------------------

The hook must feel like a trapdoor, not an introduction.

It should:

\- create immediate curiosity;

\- avoid generic openings;

\- avoid starting neutrally with the topic name;

\- reveal the subject naturally within 20–35 seconds;

\- contain at least one strong memorable line;

\- establish tonal identity;

\- open a question the rest of the script must answer.

Use the strongest hook from the evaluated versions, or create a hybrid if recommended.

Do not overexplain.

Do not begin with:

\- “Today we are going to talk about…”

\- “Have you ever wondered…”

\- “In this video…”

\- “What makes X so good?”

The first line matters.

Make it dangerous.

\------------------------------------------------------------

2\. FUNCTIONAL PRESENTATION

\------------------------------------------------------------

Orient the viewer.

Assume part of the audience knows the subject well.

Assume another part knows it only through cultural memory, famous images, reputation, memes, adaptations, or vague references.

Explain:

\- what the subject is;

\- the central situation;

\- the main forces or figures;

\- the key tension;

\- what the viewer must know before analysis.

This is not plot summary.

It should feel like:

“Here is what you need to know before the real argument begins.”

Keep the voice alive.

End with a line that opens the door to analysis.

\------------------------------------------------------------

3\. CONTEXT

\------------------------------------------------------------

Give the context that matters.

Include only what feeds the thesis.

Context may include:

\- creator background;

\- historical moment;

\- cultural pressure;

\- release/publication circumstances;

\- adaptation history;

\- reception;

\- controversies;

\- public misreadings;

\- relevant production or textual facts.

Do not list facts.

Tell context as pressure.

Every fact must earn its place by helping the viewer understand the later analysis.

If adaptations are relevant, use them as controlled contrast, not trivia.

\------------------------------------------------------------

4\. ANALYTICAL DEVELOPMENT

\------------------------------------------------------------

This is the core of the script.

It must:

\- build the argument;

\- use concrete evidence;

\- escalate in intensity;

\- include the midpoint turn;

\- integrate the strongest analytical passages from previous versions;

\- maintain rhythm and retention;

\- avoid repetition.

Use 5–7 or more specific evidence points.

Each piece of evidence must prove, complicate, or deepen the thesis.

The analysis should move in phases:

Phase 1:

Establish the visible thesis through accessible, persuasive evidence.

Phase 2:

Execute the midpoint turn.

Phase 3:

Push into the deeper, more uncomfortable reading.

The midpoint turn must be recognizable.

It should feel like the argument opening a locked door.

Do not label it mechanically.

Make it happen through the prose.

\------------------------------------------------------------

5\. COUNTERPOINT / NUANCE

\------------------------------------------------------------

The counterpoint must be strong.

State the best objection or complication.

Let it breathe.

Then respond by:

\- conceding part of it;

\- reframing it;

\- absorbing it into a more complex thesis;

\- or clarifying where the thesis holds.

Do not use weak balance.

Do not write:

“Some people may disagree.”

The counterpoint should make the script smarter.

\------------------------------------------------------------

6\. EMOTIONAL CLOSING

\------------------------------------------------------------

The closing must not summarize the video.

It should reveal what the whole analysis was circling underneath the surface.

Use the strongest emotional material from the evaluated versions, but rewrite it into one coherent final movement.

The final idea must be specific.

Avoid:

\- “Ultimately…”

\- “In conclusion…”

\- “At the end of the day…”

\- “This teaches us that…”

\- generic humanist statements;

\- calls to action;

\- channel promotion.

The final line should be memorable enough to stay with the viewer.
